Well, there are a couple of analogies that help me, at least, to model this confusion and why it exists. Humans, at one point, hard-coded the arithmetic rules that we had learned and invented a calculator to sum better than our brains could. It has fixed rules with unbounded outputs. There are calculations your calculator could do that no other human has ever asked for or completed itself. But, with machine learning models, we went one meta layer higher. We invented models that could capture even the rules that we didn't know. By definition, such a model must not be fully coded, otherwise it couldn't capture patterns beyond human programming. So, with calculators, we could understand the tool even if we couldn't do all of its sums. But, to conquer language and invention itself, you need unbounded pattern recognition, unbounded rules with unbounded outputs, such that we can neither fully understand the tool nor its outputs, of course. Extrapolating naively, our destiny would then be to solve everything and understand approximately none of it. I'm going to now bring in my second analogy, which is chess. Like a calculator, chess has rules or hard-coded affordances. In chess, the rules are bounded and the outputs explode in complexity and beauty. Humans can comprehend the rules of chess while being permanently defeated by the complexity that results. In language models, the {quote} rules that the models derive for themselves across billions of attempts to predicting the useful next token are themselves near unbounded. Hence, we are not only defeated by the output complexity, how models can defeat our longest-standing problems like the Millennium Prize challenges. More than that, we can't even grasp how many rules are being followed to do so. The humans dump in the data fuel for ignition and supply the hardware on which the models run, but then more so just bear witness to the combinatorial explosion that follows. As I kind of said, this was almost a predictable final abstraction. The patterns we couldn't grasp mastered by a tool that we don't understand and cannot predict. To overly simplify, here's why I think it seems naive to expect a human to fully understand and predict the actions of a model in five simple steps. First, just as with chess, you can't predict the board state X moves into a game against Stockfish, the hardest chess engine. With LLMs, you not only can't predict the outputs emerging through X layers of the architecture, you can't even predict the rules that led to them. Second, therefore, hoping for all such rules to be human legible and graspable is about as equivalently naive as hoping that humans will one day surpass Stockfish at chess if Stockfish was somehow running on billions of dollars of compute. Here is a clip from this channel couple of years ago arguing that like Stockfish, the O1 model from OpenAI at the time pointed toward superhuman reasoning performance. Stockfish removed the handcrafted evaluation and transitioned to a fully neural network based approach. Or put another way, by crafting its own reasoning steps and being optimized to put them together in the most effective fashion, we may end up with reasoning that we ourselves couldn't have come up with. As long as there is something out there that can grade correct versus incorrect, the performance will keep improving. Third, the only rule spotter potentially capable of fully deducing what rules an LLM is following is another machine learning model. Fourth, though, as that is a model itself, the rule it used to produce the explanation is itself indecipherable to humans. Fifth and finally, so therefore, even if we had a recursive assembly, you could say, of models explaining the rules that led to the outputs in other LLMs, and models explaining the rules that led to those explanations, ultimately humans would not grasp the ground truth rules. In a sense, then, every model circuit analyzed or neuron explained does boost our understanding, but it also boosts the chance that we forget our understanding will forever be ever further from the truth. This doesn't, by the way, prove that none of the learned rules are explainable. I think it was Neil Nanda that fully explained one rule created by a a single layer model used for modular addition. But even that for me is like a sufficiently simple end game in chess where a human could, if it was down to just a couple of pieces, probably could fully explain why Stockfish made a certain move. Nor of course am I saying that we shouldn't seek more understanding of models that probably will be useful.
